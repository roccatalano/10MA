Key ideas

1A Review of algebra

Key ideas

  • term: numbers and pronumerals connected by multiplication and division, e.g. $5x,\ 7x^2y,\ \dfrac{2a}{3},\ 7$ (a constant term)
  • coefficient: $-3$ is the coefficient of $x^2$ in $7 - 3x^2$; $1$ is the coefficient of $y$ in $y + 7x$.
  • expression: a term or terms connected by addition and subtraction, e.g. $7x,\ 3x + 2xy,\ \dfrac{x + 3}{2},\ \sqrt{2a^2 - b}$
  • equation: mathematical statements that include an equals sign, e.g. $x = 5,\ 7x - 1 = 2,\ x^2 + 2x = -4$
  • Expressions can be evaluated by substituting a value for each pronumeral (variable). Order of operations are followed.
  • Like terms have the same pronumeral part and, using addition and subtraction, can be collected to form a single term.
  • The distributive law is used to expand brackets.
  • Factorisation involves writing expressions as a product of factors. Many expressions can be factorised by taking out the highest common factor (HCF).

Key formulas

Like terms$3x - 7x + x = -3x$
$6a^2b - ba^2 = 5a^2b$   (note $a^2b = ba^2$)
$\times$ and $\div$$7 \times x \div y = \dfrac{7x}{y}$
$-6a^2b \div (ab) = \dfrac{-6a^2b}{ab} = -6a$
Distributive law$a(b + c) = ab + ac$   e.g. $2(x + 7) = 2x + 14$
$a(b - c) = ab - ac$   e.g. $-x(3 - x) = -3x + x^2$
Factorising (HCF)$3x - 12 = 3(x - 4)$
$9x^2y - 6xy + 3x = 3x(3xy - 2y + 1)$
Propertiescommutative $ab = ba$, $a + b = b + a$
associative $a \times (b \times c) = (a \times b) \times c$
identity $a \times 1 = a$, $a + 0 = a$
inverse $a \times \frac{1}{a} = 1$, $a + (-a) = 0$

Key ideas

1B Solving linear equations

Key ideas

  • A linear equation is a statement that contains an equals sign and includes constants and pronumerals with a power of 1 only.
  • A linear equation contains pronumerals with a highest power of 1.
  • An equation is true for the given values of the pronumerals when the left-hand side equals the right-hand side.
  • We solve linear equations by operating on both sides of the equation until a solution is found.
  • Useful steps in solving linear equations are: using inverse operations (backtracking), collecting like terms, expanding brackets, multiplying by the denominator.
  • Algebraic fractions can be added or subtracted by first finding the lowest common denominator (LCD) and then combining the numerators.

Key ideas

1C Linear inequalities

Key ideas

  • An inequality is a mathematical statement that uses an is less than (<), an is less than or equal to (⩽), an is greater than (>) or an is greater than or equal to (⩾) symbol. Inequalities may result in an infinite number of solutions and these can be illustrated using a number line.
  • An open circle is used for < or > where the endpoint is not included.
  • A closed circle is used for ⩽ or ⩾ where the endpoint is included.
  • Solving linear inequalities follows the same rules as solving linear equations, except: we reverse the inequality sign if we multiply or divide by a negative number; we reverse the inequality symbol if the sides are switched.

Key formulas

Reverse the sign−5<−3 is equivalent to 5>3 and if −2x<4, then x>−2.

Key ideas

1E Graphing straight lines

Key ideas

  • In two dimensions, a straight-line graph can be described by a linear equation. Common forms of such equations are y=mx+c and ax+by=d, where a, b, c, d and m are constants.
  • The gradient, m, is a number that describes the slope of a line. The gradient is the change in y per 1 unit change in x. Gradient is also referred to as the ‘rate of change of y’.
  • The intercepts are the points where the line crosses the x-axis and the y-axis. The y-intercept is the point where x=0. The x-intercept is the point where y=0.
  • The gradient of a line can be positive, negative, zero (i.e. a horizontal line) or undefined (i.e. a vertical line).
  • The gradient–intercept form of a straight line is y=mx+c, where m is the gradient and (0,c) are the coordinates of the y-intercept.
  • Two points are needed to sketch most straight-line graphs.

1F Finding the equation of a line

Key ideas

  • It is a common procedure in mathematics to find the equation (or rule) of a straight line. Once the equation of a line is determined, it can be used to find the exact coordinates of other points on the line.
  • Horizontal lines have the equation y=c, where c is the y-coordinate of the y-intercept.
  • Vertical lines have the equation x=k, where k is the x-coordinate of the x-intercept.
  • Given the gradient (m) and the y-intercept (0,c), use y=mx+c to state the equation of the line.
  • To find the equation of a line when given any two points, find the gradient (m), then: substitute a point to find c in y=mx+c, or use y−y1​=m(x−x1​).

Key ideas

1G Length and midpoint of a line segment

Key ideas

  • Two important features of a line segment (or line interval) are its length and midpoint. The length can be found using Pythagoras’ theorem and the midpoint can be found by considering the midpoints of the horizontal and vertical components of the line segment.
  • The length of a line segment d (or distance between two points (x1​,y1​) and (x2​,y2​)) comes from Pythagoras’ theorem where the distance d is the length of the hypotenuse of the right-angled triangle formed.
  • The midpoint M of a line segment between (x1​,y1​) and (x2​,y2​) is the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates.

Key formulas

Length (distance)d=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​
MidpointM=(2x1​+x2​​, 2y1​+y2​​)

Key ideas

1L Regions on the Cartesian plane

Key ideas

  • Linear inequalities can also have two variables: 2x−3y⩾5 and y<3−x are two examples. The solutions to such inequalities will be an infinite set of points on a plane sitting on one side of a line. This region is also called a half plane.
  • The solution to a linear inequality with two variables is illustrated using a shaded region of the Cartesian plane.
  • If the equation is of the form ax+by=d, it is usually simpler to test a point, for example (0,0), to see which side of the line is to be included in the region.
  • When two or more linear inequalities are sketched on the same set of axes, the regions overlap and form an intersecting region. The set of points inside the intersecting region will be the solution to the simultaneous inequalities.
  • To help define the intersecting region correctly, you should determine and label the point of intersection.

Key formulas

y⩾mx+cDraw a solid line (as it is included in the region) and shade above.
y>mx+cDraw a broken line (as it is not included in the region) and shade above.
y⩽mx+cDraw a solid line (as it is included in the region) and shade below.
y<mx+cDraw a broken line (as it is not included in the region) and shade below.

Key ideas

1I Simultaneous equations using substitution

Key ideas

  • When we try to find a solution to a set of equations rather than just a single equation, we say that we are solving simultaneous equations. For two linear simultaneous equations we are interested in finding the point where the graphs of the two equations meet.
  • Solving two simultaneous equations involves finding a solution that satisfies both equations. When two straight lines are not parallel, there will be a single (unique) solution.
  • The substitution method is usually used when at least one of the equations has one pronumeral as the subject. For example, y=3x+2 or x=3y−1. By substituting one equation into the other, a single equation in terms of one pronumeral is formed and can then be solved.

Find y: y=3(1)+4=7. So the solution is x=1 and y=7. The point (1,7) is the intersection point of the graphs of the two relations.

  • When there is no matching pair (as in the second example below) one or both of the equations can be multiplied by a chosen factor.

Key ideas

  • When a problem involves two unknown variables, simultaneous equations can be used to find the solution to the problem, provided that the two pronumerals can be identified and two equations can be written from the problem description.

Key formulas

When solving problems with two unknowns
  1. Define a variable for each unknown.
  2. Write down two equations from the information given.
  3. Solve the equations simultaneously to find the solution (using the method of substitution or elimination).
  4. Interpret the solution and answer the question in words.
